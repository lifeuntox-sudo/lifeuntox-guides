## The first 72 hours decide everything

Every serious disruption follows the same pattern. The power flickers or the news breaks, and people rush the stores within the hour. The shelves that feed a whole town are stripped before most families have finished their coffee. The water, the batteries and the calorie-dense food go first.

The reassuring truth is that the first 72 hours are also the ones you can fully control. [Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/kit) tells every household to keep several days of food and water on hand, and the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/how-to-create-and-store-an-emergency-water-supply.html) sets the floor at three days. Closing that gap is simpler and cheaper than you have been led to believe.

An old survival rule of thumb sets the order. A body lasts around three minutes without air, three days without water and three weeks without food. So water comes first, calories come second, and everything else supports those two. Build in that order and you never waste money on the wrong thing.

This blueprint hands you the whole plan in plain terms. You get the water plan that keeps your family safe, the staples that store for years, and the gear that carries you through a dark, quiet house. Build it once, and the next time the headlines turn frightening, you stay home.

:::warn Plan for the power being off, not dimmed
Five mistakes leave families exposed: a phone with no way to charge it, a generator with no stored fuel, food the family will not eat, no plan for pets, infants or medication, and everything kept in the freezer. A full freezer holds its temperature for [about 48 hours](https://www.ready.gov/power-outages) with the door shut, and a fridge for about four. Check your own home against these five today, and fix the first gap you find this week.
:::

## Part 1: Water, your first priority

You can go weeks without food and only days without clean water, so every supply begins here. The official guidance is [one gallon per person per day](https://www.ready.gov/water), for drinking and sanitation. For a family of four over 72 hours, that is twelve gallons stored and ready before anything goes wrong.

The CDC says to store more for pregnant women, anyone who is sick, pets and hot climates. If you have the room, it suggests working up to a two-week supply.

Storing water right takes four habits:

- **Buy sealed bottled water, or use food-grade containers.** Bottled water in its sealed original container is the simplest choice. For your own, use food-grade jugs with a tight lid. Never reuse a container that held bleach, pesticide or any other chemical.
- **Keep it cool and dark.** The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/how-to-create-and-store-an-emergency-water-supply.html) says to store water at 50 to 70°F, out of direct sunlight, and away from gasoline and pesticides. Label each container "drinking water" with the date.
- **Replace it every six months.** That rule covers water you bottled yourself. For store-bought water, follow the date on the pack.
- **Fill the bathtub early.** At the first warning, fill tubs and large pots. This water handles cleaning and flushing, which frees your stored water for drinking.

When your stored water runs low, four methods turn questionable water into safe water. Keep more than one on hand, because each one covers a situation the others do not. If the water is cloudy, let it settle first and pour it through a clean cloth or a coffee filter.

1. **Rolling boil.** The most reliable method of all. Heat kills bacteria, viruses and parasites. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/water-emergency/about/index.html) says to boil for one full minute, or three minutes above 6,500 feet, then let the water cool.
2. **Household bleach.** Plain unscented bleach disinfects when you cannot boil. For bleach with 5 to 9 percent sodium hypochlorite on the label, add 8 drops per gallon, stir, and wait 30 minutes. Double the dose if the water is cloudy or very cold. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/emergency-disinfection-drinking-water) says never to use scented, colour-safe or cleaner-added bleach, and to use a bottle less than a year old.
3. **Portable filter.** A filter with a pore size of 0.3 micron or smaller removes bacteria and parasites as you drink. [Sawyer](https://www.sawyer.com/) and [LifeStraw](https://lifestraw.com/) are widely sold options. Filters do not remove viruses, so the CDC says to disinfect the water after you filter it.
4. **Purification tablets.** A lightweight backup that stores for years in any kit. Choose chlorine dioxide tablets. The CDC says iodine and chlorine tablets do not kill Cryptosporidium, a parasite that causes severe diarrhea, and chlorine dioxide does when used as directed.

Store twelve gallons and one backup method, and the hardest part of the first 72 hours is already handled.

## Part 2: The calorie-dense pantry

In a crisis your body needs calories far more than variety. The smartest staples deliver the most energy per pound and store for years. [Ready.gov](https://www.ready.gov/food) adds one rule above all the others: choose foods your family will eat.

The long shelf lives below come from [Utah State University Extension](https://extension.usu.edu/preserve-the-harvest/files/Food-Storage-Booklet.pdf). They apply to dry food sealed in airtight containers with oxygen absorbers and kept cool. A bag from the store that sits in the cupboard is for rotating: eat from it and replace it.

1. **White rice.** Cheap, filling and calorie-dense, the backbone of any supply. Sealed and kept cool, it stores well for [25 to 30 years](https://extension.usu.edu/preserve-the-harvest/research/storing-white-rice). Once you open a container, use it within one to two years.
2. **Dried beans and lentils.** Protein and fibre in the same bag, and they pair with rice for a complete meal. Sealed the same way, beans were still acceptable for emergency use after up to 30 years.
3. **Rolled oats.** Fast energy with no cooking required: soak them in water and eat. Stored airtight with oxygen absorbers, oats keep for up to 30 years.
4. **Peanut butter.** Dense calories, protein and fat, ready to eat straight from the jar. It is on Ready.gov's own list. Rotate it by the date on the jar.
5. **Canned meats and fish.** Protein that needs no fridge, from tuna to chicken to salmon. The [USDA](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/shelf-stable-food) says low-acid canned foods like these keep their best quality for 2 to 5 years. Throw out any can that is bulging, leaking or badly dented.
6. **Cooking oil and honey.** Concentrated calories in a small space. Oil turns rancid with time, so use it in your normal cooking and replace it. Honey keeps for years, and it is never for a baby under one year old.
7. **Salt and electrolytes.** Salt keeps indefinitely and makes plain meals bearable. Electrolyte packets replace what the body loses through sweat and sickness.

Add what your own household needs on top. Ready.gov lists ready-to-feed formula for infants, food and extra water for pets, and a few comfort foods, because morale matters in a dark house.

When the power goes out, eat in the right order. Start with the fridge, which keeps food cold for [about four hours](https://www.ready.gov/food) if it stays shut. Move to the freezer next, and save the pantry for last. Throw out any meat, fish, eggs or leftovers that have sat above 40°F for two hours or more.

## Part 3: The off-grid essentials

Food and water keep you alive. This gear keeps you warm, informed and able to function when the power stays off. Everything here is affordable, stores for years, and fits in a single tote you can grab in seconds.

**Light and power.**

- Headlamps and flashlights for every person, with a deep supply of spare batteries, so your hands stay free.
- A power bank and a small solar charger to keep a phone alive for emergency alerts.
- A battery or hand-crank radio that receives [NOAA Weather Radio](https://www.weather.gov/nwr/), which broadcasts official warnings 24 hours a day when the networks fail.

**Warmth and cooking.**

- A wool blanket or a sleeping bag for each person, since a dark house loses heat fast.
- A camp stove or grill with fuel, and a manual can opener for your tins.
- Lighters and waterproof matches. Reach for a flashlight before a candle, and never leave a flame burning in an empty room.

:::warn Stoves, grills and generators run outdoors only
Carbon monoxide is a gas you cannot see or smell, and the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html) says it kills more than 400 Americans every year. Run any camp stove, charcoal grill or generator outside, at least 20 feet from windows and doors. Never heat your home with a gas oven. Fit a battery-powered carbon monoxide detector near every sleeping area before you need it.
:::

**Health and documents.**

- A stocked first aid kit, plus a spare supply of any prescription your family takes. Have a plan for medicine that needs a fridge.
- Cash in small bills, because card readers and ATMs go down with the grid.
- Copies of IDs, insurance and key documents in a waterproof bag, along with a paper list of emergency contacts.
- Diapers, wipes and formula for a baby, and food for every pet.

## Start this weekend, not next crisis

:::protocol
You do not need to buy everything at once. Add one category to your normal shop each week, water first, then food, then gear. Within a month the supply is built.

1. **Week 1: water.** Buy or fill one gallon per person per day for three days. Add a bottle of plain unscented bleach, and write the date on everything.
2. **Week 2: food.** Add rice, beans, oats, peanut butter and canned meat or fish to your usual shop. Buy what the family already eats.
3. **Week 3: gear.** Fill one tote with headlamps, batteries, a power bank, a radio, a manual can opener, a first aid kit and cash.
4. **Week 4: paper and practice.** Copy your documents into a waterproof bag, print the grab list, and tape it inside the pantry door. Set a reminder to review it twice a year.
:::

Within a month you will have quietly built the one thing money cannot buy once the shelves are empty, which is certainty. The families who stay calm are the ones who prepared before the headlines. Start with the water this weekend.
