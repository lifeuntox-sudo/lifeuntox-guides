## Why your ice cream is suddenly under the microscope

Something remarkable is happening in the freezer aisle, and most families have no idea it is unfolding right now. The ice cream industry is in the middle of the biggest ingredient overhaul in its modern history.

In July 2025 the International Dairy Foods Association announced that [around 40 companies](https://www.idfa.org/idfa-ice-cream-commitment), making more than 90 percent of the ice cream sold in the United States, pledged to remove seven petroleum-based dyes from their tubs. The dyes on the chopping block are Red 3, Red 40, Green 3, Blue 1, Blue 2, Yellow 5 and Yellow 6.

The pressure came from above. In April 2025 the FDA announced a plan to [phase out petroleum-based synthetic dyes](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/hhs-fda-phase-out-petroleum-based-synthetic-dyes-nations-food-supply) from the whole food supply, three months after it [revoked Red 3](https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-revoke-authorization-use-red-no-3-food-and-ingested-drugs). California's own review found these dyes [can affect attention and behaviour](https://oehha.ca.gov/risk-assessment/report/health-effects-assessment-potential-neurobehavioral-effects-synthetic-food-dyes) in some children.

Here is the catch. The industry deadline is the end of 2027, the pledge is voluntary, and the change is rolling out flavour by flavour. The pint you buy today can be the new formula, or the old one on the same shelf.

That is exactly why naming names is useful right now. This cheat sheet sorts the real brands into three groups, adds a natural-colour decoder, and ends with a rule you can run in ten seconds.

:::warn A colour and a number is the red flag
Any ingredient that reads Red 40, Yellow 5, Blue 1 or similar is a synthetic dye; a number beside a colour is the clearest sign on the label. The FDA calls them [certified color additives](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/color-additives-questions-and-answers-consumers). If you see one, put the tub back and pick a flavour from List 1 below, or the plainest vanilla or chocolate from any organic brand.
:::

## List 1: Already dye-free

These brands have relied on natural colours, or no added colour at all, for years. Many built their whole identity around clean labels, so you can reach for them with confidence today. Glance at the specific flavour, since mix-ins vary.

- **Häagen-Dazs, classic line.** The original flavours are famously short lists: cream, milk, sugar, egg yolks and vanilla, with no artificial colours or flavours.
- **Ben & Jerry's.** Publicly committed to naturally sourced colours and flavours across the range.
- **Tillamook.** Its ingredient pledge promises Grade A milk and cream with naturally sourced colours and flavours.
- **Talenti Gelato.** Minimal ingredient lists with no artificial colours or fillers, and every ingredient named plainly on the lid.
- **Van Leeuwen.** Colours with real ingredients such as beet juice rather than petroleum-based dyes.
- **Jeni's Splendid.** A clean-label maker that lists recognisable ingredients and skips synthetic colours.
- **Straus, Alden's Organic and Three Twins.** Certified organic makers. USDA organic rules [do not allow synthetic colours](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-7/subtitle-B/chapter-I/subchapter-M/part-205/subpart-G/section-205.605), so the organic seal does the checking for you.
- **So Delicious, non-dairy.** Plant-based lines built for label readers, free of titanium dioxide and synthetic dyes.

The fast shortcut when you are in a hurry: the plainest flavour from an organic or clean-label brand. A vanilla bean or a dark chocolate from any maker above is almost always a safe bet.

## List 2: Changing by 2027

This is where most household names sit right now. These companies have publicly committed to removing the seven dyes, and the change is rolling out across their ranges over the next couple of years.

- **Nestlé brands.** Committed to the earliest deadline of the group, aiming to remove artificial colours by the middle of 2026.
- **Wells Enterprises: Blue Bunny, Halo Top, Bomb Pop.** Publicly phasing out artificial colours across its whole portfolio.
- **Turkey Hill.** Joined the industry commitment in person, so expect reformulated tubs through 2027.
- **Dreyer's and Edy's.** Classic flavours already advertise no artificial colours, with the rest of the range following.
- **The wider IDFA membership.** Roughly 40 companies, all pledged to remove the seven dyes by the end of 2027.

Transition takes time. A brand can be fully committed while some of its brightest flavours still carry the old formula, and a reformulated tub and an older one can sit side by side for months. Look for the phrase "colored with fruit and vegetable juices": as brands reformulate, they put it on the front of the pack.

## List 3: Check twice for now

These are the products most likely to still contain synthetic dyes or other additives today. None of them are villains, and many will change soon, but right now they are the ones to turn over and read before they reach the cart.

- **Kroger Deluxe.** Ingredient lists show titanium dioxide as a whitener plus artificial flavours in several tubs.
- **Perry's Ice Cream.** A number of flavours still list artificial ingredients and preservatives.
- **Blue Bunny novelty and character treats.** The parent company is reformulating, yet many bright novelties still carry synthetic dyes for now.
- **Neon flavours from any brand.** Bubblegum, superman, cotton candy and rainbow shades are the most likely to rely on Red 40, Blue 1 and Yellow 5.
- **Licensed cartoon push-pops and character bars.** Kid-targeted novelties use the boldest synthetic colours to stand out in the freezer case.
- **Store-brand and value sherbet.** Vivid orange, green and pink sherbets often take their colour from dyes rather than fruit.

The colour clue: the more unnaturally vivid the ice cream, the more likely it contains a synthetic dye. Real fruit and plant colours look softer. Electric blue and neon green almost never come from nature.

## The natural-colour decoder

When a brand reformulates, it swaps synthetic dyes for colours drawn from real food. The names look unfamiliar at first, so here is the translation. Seeing any of these on the label is a good sign.

:::table
| On the label | What it really is |
|---|---|
| Beet juice or beetroot extract | A pink or red from real beets, in place of Red 40 |
| Annatto | A warm yellow-orange from achiote seeds, in place of Yellow 5 and 6 |
| Turmeric | A golden yellow from the spice root |
| Spirulina extract | A blue-green from algae, in place of Blue 1 |
| Butterfly pea flower | A natural blue from a flower petal, [approved by the FDA](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-three-food-colors-natural-sources) for wider use in 2025 |
| Purple or black carrot | A deep purple for berry and grape shades |
| Fruit and vegetable juice | The clean-label catch-all: the colour comes from real produce |
:::

Natural colours behave differently in the freezer, so a naturally coloured strawberry ice cream looks paler than the neon pink you remember. That softer shade is the sign of a cleaner formula, not a lesser one.

## The additives beyond the dyes

Synthetic dyes get the headlines, and a few other additives are worth knowing while you have the tub in your hand.

- **Titanium dioxide.** A whitener the European Union [no longer considers safe](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/news/titanium-dioxide-e171-no-longer-considered-safe-when-used-food-additive) as a food additive. It shows up in some value ice creams to make them look whiter.
- **Carrageenan.** A seaweed thickener that some people find hard on digestion. Many clean brands have dropped it, so its absence is a good sign.
- **High fructose corn syrup.** A cheap sweetener that clean-label brands replace with cane sugar. A short ingredient list rarely includes it.
- **Vague "natural and artificial flavors".** When a label hides behind this phrase instead of naming the flavour, it is worth a second thought.

Going dye-free does not mean the end of exciting treats. Swap neon bubblegum for a naturally coloured strawberry swirl from a clean brand, rainbow sherbet for a real-fruit sorbet, character push-pops for spirulina or butterfly-pea pops, and candy-loaded pints for a clean chocolate or cookie flavour where the mix-ins are real food.

## The 10-second freezer-aisle rule

:::protocol
You will not always have this guide open on your phone, so here is a system you can run in about ten seconds on any pint, anywhere.

1. **Scan for a colour and a number.** Red 40, Yellow 5, Blue 1 or similar means a synthetic dye. Back on the shelf.
2. **Look for the natural-colour language.** "Colored with fruit and vegetable juices", or any name from the decoder, means the brand has made the switch.
3. **Trust the shade.** If the colour belongs in a highlighter rather than a kitchen, treat it as a check-twice product and read the full list.
4. **Default to plain.** When in doubt, the simplest vanilla or chocolate from an organic or List 1 brand is the safe pick, every time.
:::
