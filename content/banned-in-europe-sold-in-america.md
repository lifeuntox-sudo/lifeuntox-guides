## The two-shelf problem

Picture two boxes of the same brand-name cereal, one on a shelf in Paris and the other in Pittsburgh. Same logo, same cartoon on the front, same promise of a good morning. Turn them over, though, and the ingredient lists do not match. The European box has been quietly reformulated to meet stricter rules, and the American one has not.

That gap is the whole story. For decades, Europe has pulled certain additives from its food supply over cancer, hormone and children's health concerns, while those same ingredients have stayed legal, and common, on American shelves.

It comes down to one word: proof, and who has to provide it. In Europe, the burden sits with the manufacturer. Under the [EU's general food law](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32002R0178), regulators use what is called the precautionary principle. When the evidence is uncertain, the additive waits outside the food supply rather than inside it.

The United States runs the opposite way. A rule known as [GRAS](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-ingredients-packaging/generally-recognized-safe-gras), short for Generally Recognized As Safe, lets companies decide for themselves that an ingredient is fine, sometimes without ever telling the Food and Drug Administration.

Here is the encouraging part. That system is finally shifting. [Red 3 lost its approval in 2025](https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-revoke-authorization-use-red-no-3-food-and-ingested-drugs), the FDA is [pushing six more dyes out](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/hhs-fda-phase-out-petroleum-based-synthetic-dyes-nations-food-supply), and [California](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB418) has passed its own bans. Yet almost all of these ingredients remain on shelves today, because a phase-out on paper takes years to reach the checkout. This list closes that gap for you.

:::warn "Banned in Europe" does not mean proven dangerous
It usually means European regulators were not willing to wait for certainty while people kept eating it. The American question is different: it asks whether harm has already been proven, and by the time that proof arrives, an ingredient has often been in the food supply for forty years. So treat this list as a reason to choose differently, never a reason to panic. Every additive below has a cleaner version of the same product sitting a shelf away.
:::

## Section A: Artificial dyes

These are the brightest offenders, literally. They add no flavour and no nutrition. They exist to make food look more fun, and they are the additives European regulators have treated most cautiously, especially around children.

**Red 3 (erythrosine).** Found in some candies, maraschino cherries, strawberry-flavoured products and icings. Linked to thyroid tumours in animal studies, and restricted in Europe for decades. The [FDA revoked it in January 2025](https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-revoke-authorization-use-red-no-3-food-and-ingested-drugs), yet it stays legal on shelves until makers reformulate by [January 2027](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-ingredients-packaging/fda-encourages-food-manufacturers-accelerate-phasing-out-use-fdc-red-no-3-foods-2027-deadline). Check the label on maraschino cherries, candy corn and seasonal sweets.

**Red 40, Yellow 5, Yellow 6, Blue 1, Blue 2 and Green 3.** Found in brightly coloured cereals, candies, sports drinks, sodas, snack chips, icings and jellies. Associated with hyperactivity and attention problems in some children. In Europe, foods with these dyes must carry a [warning label](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2008/1333/oj) saying they may affect activity and attention in children. In the US they stay approved, now under a [voluntary phase-out](https://www.fda.gov/food/color-additives-information-consumers/tracking-food-industry-pledges-remove-petroleum-based-food-dyes) the industry has been asked to finish by the end of 2027. Check the label on Skittles, M&M's, Mountain Dew, Doritos and Gatorade, though many are reformulating through 2026.

The shelf check for this whole section takes one second. Any ingredient written as a colour plus a number is a synthetic dye.

## Section B: Bread, buns and flour

The chemicals in this group do not colour your food. They speed up factory baking and make dough easier to handle at scale, which is exactly why they turn up in the cheapest, most mass-produced loaves.

**Potassium bromate.** Found in some commercial breads, rolls, bagels, flour and frozen pizza crusts. A dough strengthener that the [International Agency for Research on Cancer](https://monographs.iarc.who.int/list-of-classifications) classes as a possible human carcinogen (Group 2B). Not permitted in Europe, and banned in the UK, Canada and China. Still [legal in US flour](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-136/subpart-B/section-136.110), with a [California ban](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB418) arriving in 2027. Check the label on breakfast sandwiches, kaiser rolls, pastry dough and store-brand French bread pizzas.

**Azodicarbonamide (ADA).** Found in sandwich breads, burger buns and other mass-produced baked goods. Nicknamed the yoga mat chemical for its industrial uses. It can form compounds flagged as possible carcinogens when baked, which is why Europe does not allow it in food. The [FDA permits it](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/azodicarbonamide-ada-frequently-asked-questions) up to a set limit. Subway removed it in 2014 after public pressure.

**Chemically bleached flour.** Found in standard white flour, cake mixes and many packaged baked goods. Europe does not allow these chemical bleaching agents. The US treats them as acceptable, so the word bleached on a bag of flour signals a process Europe rejected. Choose unbleached flour, which sits right next to it at the same price.

## Section C: Drinks

This section is short, and it carries good news. It shows that the two-shelf gap can close when enough people pay attention.

**Brominated vegetable oil (BVO).** Once found in Mountain Dew and Gatorade, then only in smaller regional sodas. Originally patented as a flame retardant, with concerns about bromine building up in the body. Europe and Japan banned it long ago. The [FDA revoked it in 2024](https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-revokes-regulation-allowing-use-brominated-vegetable-oil-bvo-food), so it is now leaving American drinks, a rare case of the US catching up. Check the label on citrus sodas from small brands.

## Section D: Meat and dairy

This group is harder to spot, because it does not appear on an ingredient list at all. It is about how the animal was raised, which is why the fix here is about choosing differently rather than reading differently.

**rBGH or rBST (growth hormone).** Found in conventional, non-organic milk and dairy from treated cows. A lab-made hormone used to push milk production. [Banned in Europe](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:31999D0879), Canada, Australia, Japan and New Zealand over welfare and safety questions. [Legal in the US](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/product-safety-information/bovine-somatotropin-bst), which is why organic and no-rBST dairy exist as a workaround. Choose anything marked USDA Organic or no rBST.

**Ractopamine.** Found in conventional pork, and some beef and turkey. A drug given to animals to add lean muscle before slaughter. [Banned in Europe](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:31996L0022) and around 160 countries over heart and residue concerns. [Legal in US meat production](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-E/part-556/subpart-B/section-556.570). Choose USDA Organic meat, or labels that say raised without ractopamine.

**Chlorine-washed chicken.** Found in conventional US chicken. Europe's [hygiene rules](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32004R0853) do not allow washing poultry in chlorine, because it can mask poor hygiene earlier in the process. It stays [routine in the US](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/policy/fsis-directives/7120.1). Choose USDA Organic or air-chilled chicken; our [Clean Meat Directory](/guide-clean-meat-directory) names ten brands.

## Section E: Preservatives, whiteners and fillers

This is the longest group, and the easiest to miss, because these ingredients are designed to be invisible. They keep fats from turning, make whites whiter, and extend shelf life far past what real food allows.

**Titanium dioxide (E171).** Found in candies, chewing gum, frostings, coffee creamers and some supplement coatings. A whitening agent [Europe banned in food in 2022](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/63/oj) after its food safety authority said it [could no longer be considered safe](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/news/titanium-dioxide-e171-no-longer-considered-safe-when-used-food-additive), over concerns it may damage DNA. [Still legal in the US](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-73/subpart-A/section-73.575) as a colour additive. Check the label on sour candies, powdered donuts and snack cakes. Skittles removed it in 2024.

**BHA and BHT.** Found in breakfast cereals, cured meats, chips, chewing gum and butter. Preservatives that stop fats going rancid. The US National Toxicology Program lists BHA as [reasonably anticipated to be a human carcinogen](https://ntp.niehs.nih.gov/sites/default/files/ntp/roc/content/profiles/butylatedhydroxyanisole.pdf), and Europe restricts both. The US keeps them under the GRAS label. Check the label on cereals such as Froot Loops, Frosted Flakes and Cinnamon Toast Crunch, plus many chewing gums.

**Propylparaben.** Found in some tortillas, baked goods and pastries. A preservative that acts on hormones, which Europe removed from food in 2006. [Legal in the US](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-184/subpart-B/section-184.1670), with [California banning it](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB418) from 2027. Check the label on flour tortillas and packaged snack cakes.

**Propylene oxide.** Used to treat some conventional almonds, cocoa and spices against bacteria. The [EPA classes it](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-09/documents/propylene-oxide.pdf) as a probable human carcinogen. Not permitted for food use in Europe, still used in the US. Organic almonds are not treated this way, so the word organic is the whole check.

**Olestra (Olean).** Found historically in fat-free chips, now largely gone from shelves. A fake fat that blocks absorption of some vitamins and causes digestive problems. Banned in the UK, Canada and across Europe. [Still permitted in the US](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-172/subpart-I/section-172.867).

## How to spot them in ten seconds

:::protocol
You do not need to memorise this list. You need three habits that catch almost everything on it without slowing down your shop.

1. **Scan for colours and numbers.** Any ingredient written as a colour plus a number, such as Red 40 or Yellow 5, is a synthetic dye. The same goes for E-numbers in the 100s.
2. **Flag the long chemical names.** If a word reads like it belongs in a lab rather than a kitchen, treat it as a question. Potassium bromate, azodicarbonamide, propylparaben and titanium dioxide are the four worth knowing by sight.
3. **Read the word before flour and the word after conventional.** Bleached flour and conventional dairy or meat are the two phrases that quietly signal this whole list. For dairy and meat, choosing [USDA Organic](https://www.ams.usda.gov/rules-regulations/organic) removes the hormones and the growth drugs in one move, because organic rules do not permit them.
:::

You do not have to clear your whole kitchen this weekend. Pick the single product you buy most often, bread, cereal or your usual snack, and find one cleaner version next time you shop. Repeat that once a week, and in two months your cart looks completely different, without a single dramatic change.
