## Part 1: What counts as synthetic food

Two very different technologies get lumped together under scary headlines. They are worth telling apart, because only one of them involves growing actual animal tissue.

**Cell-cultivated meat and seafood** is real animal muscle or fat, grown from a small sample of animal cells in a steel tank called a bioreactor, with no slaughter involved. The finished product is biologically meat. This is the category the [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-ingredients-packaging/human-food-made-cultured-animal-cells) and USDA review, and the one this tracker follows most closely.

**3D-printed meat and seafood** is a manufacturing method, not an ingredient. Machines layer proteins into the shape and texture of a fillet or a steak. Almost every 3D-printed product on the market today is built from plants or fungi, not animal cells, so it is closer to a veggie burger than to cultivated salmon.

A simple kitchen test cuts through the noise. If a food was grown in a field, a pasture or the sea, and your grandmother would recognise how it was made, it is not synthetic. Frozen vegetables, canned beans, plain yogurt and a normal piece of chicken all pass.

As of October 2026, only five companies have made it through the federal pathway for cell-cultivated products. The FDA reviews the cells and the process, and for meat and poultry the USDA then inspects the plant and approves the label. That is the entire approved field in the United States:

1. **[UPSIDE Foods](https://upsidefoods.com/).** Cultivated chicken. FDA and USDA cleared in 2023.
2. **[GOOD Meat](https://www.goodmeat.co/)**, part of Eat Just. Cultivated chicken. FDA and USDA cleared in 2023.

## Part 2: Where it is actually sold

This is the heart of the guide: every place synthetic meat and seafood is sold or served in the United States right now. Compare this list to what you see online, and the gap tells its own story.

**Grocery stores.** [Sprouts Farmers Market](https://www.sprouts.com/) became the first US grocery chain to put cultivated meat on its shelves, starting at its Oakland, California store. The product is Mission Barns pork, sold as branded meatballs and bacon. These are hybrid items: cultivated pork fat blended with plant protein, not a slab of pure lab-grown muscle. The products carry their own brand name rather than being mixed into the regular meat case.

**Restaurants.** The two chicken restaurants that first served cultivated meat in 2023, Bar Crenn in San Francisco and China Chilcano in Washington, DC, have since wound those menu items down. The active venues today are tastings on the West Coast:

**3D-printed products.** [Revo Foods](https://revofoods.com/) is the name to know. The company sells 3D-printed fillets, including a salmon-style product, built from mycoprotein, which is a protein grown from fungi, rather than animal cells. [Steakholder Foods](https://www.steakholderfoods.com/) supplies the printing technology itself to other manufacturers. Because these are plant and fungi based, they sit in the vegetarian aisle, not the meat case.

Add it all up and synthetic animal meat sits in one grocery chain in one state and a handful of restaurants on the West Coast. If you shop at a normal supermarket outside California, you are not buying this by accident.

## Part 3: Where it is banned, and how it is labeled

Two things protect your right to choose: state law, and labeling rules. Both are moving quickly, and both are on your side more than the headlines suggest.

Seven states have passed laws banning the sale of cultivated meat, which means a store in those states cannot legally stock it. [Florida](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2024/1084) went first in 2024. Alabama, Mississippi, Montana, Indiana, Nebraska and [Texas](https://capitol.texas.gov/BillLookup/History.aspx?LegSess=89R&Bill=SB261) followed in 2024 and 2025. Indiana's law is a two-year moratorium that runs to June 2027. Florida's ban has been challenged in court by UPSIDE Foods, so expect this list to keep changing.

If you live in one of these seven states, cultivated meat is not legally for sale near you at all.

Where it is sold, synthetic meat is required to identify itself. The USDA approves every cultivated meat and poultry label before it goes on sale, under its [labeling policies](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/inspection/compliance-guidance/labeling/labeling-policies), and several states require extra wording such as cell-cultivated or cell-cultured. You will not find it disguised inside a normal pack of chicken.

:::protocol
Three checks tell you in ten seconds whether a product is cultivated, printed or conventional.

1. **Read the front of pack.** Cultivated products carry USDA-approved labeling, and in several states the words cell-cultivated or cell-cultured must appear. No such words means conventional food.
2. **Know the five brand names.** UPSIDE Foods, GOOD Meat, Wildtype, Mission Barns and Believer Meats are the only approved cultivated brands. If none of those names appear, it is conventional food.
3. **Watch the health halo.** Words like clean, sustainable or next generation are marketing. Flip the pack over and read what it actually contains.
:::

## What to watch next

This tracker is a snapshot of a market that is still forming. Rather than reacting to every viral post, watch for the three signals that would actually mean a wider rollout is coming.

- **Production scale.** Approved companies are still small. A real jump in output is the first sign of broader availability.
- **National supply deals.** Today it is one chain in one state. A supply agreement with a national grocery brand would change the picture overnight.
- **State law outcomes.** The bans and the lawsuits around them will decide where this can and cannot be sold. Those rulings are worth following.

Until those move, synthetic meat stays exactly where this guide places it: approved, labeled, and easy to avoid for anyone who wants to. This week, pick up any pack of meat you buy and read the front for a brand name. That one habit is all the protection you need.
